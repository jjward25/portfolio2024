The 10 principles

The reasoning behind the program.

01Objective HierarchyExtreme usable ROM → strength through ROM → movement mastery → athletic force → conditioning → aesthetics.⌄

What We Are Optimizing, in Order

  1. Extreme Usable ROM: front and middle splits, full pike/pancake, and capable spinal, hip and shoulder movement.
  2. Strength Through ROM: heavy loading at long muscle lengths and difficult joint angles, alongside deliberate strengthening of often-neglected muscles and supporting functions. Examples include rectus femoris via reverse Nordics; deep knee/ankle control via ATG split squats; hip flexors, adductors, rotator cuff and tibialis work.
  3. Integrated Mastery: handstand push-ups, front levers, muscle-ups and a full gymnastics bridge.
  4. Athletic Force: generate, absorb, redirect and stabilize force in real movement.
  5. Conditioning: sustain repeated efforts and recover between them.
  6. Aesthetics: muscularity, proportions and balanced full-body development.

Joint/tendon comfort, symmetry and recovery are constraints across all six tiers, not objectives to consider only after the others.

Success is not merely reaching a position. It is entering it actively, owning it under load, transitioning out of it and retaining the ability to move quickly.

02The Five CapacitiesStrong short → strong long → actively enter extreme ROM → stabilize there → produce force quickly.⌄

These five capabilities should be covered across the whole week, not with five isolated movements for every muscle. A deep Bulgarian trains long-length strength, joint ROM and unilateral stability simultaneously.

Force absorption matters: controlled eccentrics and deceleration help turn strength into usable sport performance rather than just heavy gym numbers.

03Integrated Movement MasterySeparate muscle qualities only matter when they coordinate into demanding whole-body skills.⌄

Why Skills Have Dedicated Space

  • Handstand Push-Up: inverted pressing, overhead range, scapular elevation and trunk stacking.
  • Front Lever: straight-arm shoulder and lat strength, body-line rigidity and leverage.
  • Muscle-Up: high-speed pulling, transition coordination and pressing.
  • Full Gymnastics Bridge: shoulder flexion, thoracic/spinal extension, hip extension and whole-chain control.

None is guaranteed by a strong isolated muscle. Skill practice therefore comes fresh, before fatiguing strength work, using regressions that preserve form. Tuesday is deliberately lower fatigue, while Wednesday and Friday host harder progressions.

Good Idea for a Fitness App: track skill milestones (assistance, leverage, hold time, clean ROM and alignment) separately from barbell or dumbbell load.

04Effort and Set ArchitectureTrack 1–2 heavy strength sets on core lifts; add one lighter hypertrophy cooldown without excess fatigue.⌄

Why We Adopted The Top-Set / Back-Off Model

For 2–3 core exercises per strength day: use an easy preparation set and any necessary low-fatigue ramp-up, then 1–2 heavy strength sets (~3–6 reps, generally 1–2 RIR), followed by one controlled hypertrophy cooldown (~8–15 reps, generally 1–2 RIR). The heavy strength sets are the primary tracked progression; the lighter set adds useful hypertrophy without repeating maximal loading. Aim to recover for the next strength exposure, often 48–72 hours later, while adjusting for individual recovery and sport fatigue.

We aim to recover for the next strength exposure (often 48–72 hours), but recovery is observed, not guaranteed. Heavy failure can create disproportionate fatigue and compromise extreme-ROM mechanics. A second heavy set is an option only if comparable sessions stall while recovery is good.

We avoid fatiguing 30-rep warm-ups. Instead, rehearse the movement lightly and make a few low-rep load jumps before the top set. Not every exercise gets this format: the adaptation determines the set structure.

05Tendon and Joint DurabilityProgressive dynamic loading, selective slow reps/holds, healthy shoulder mechanics and force absorption.⌄

Tendon Emphasis Without Another Workout

Normal heavy training and sport already load tendons. We add a modest emphasis to roughly two selected exercises per strength session, not a maximal hold after every exercise. The exercise selection stays the same across Week A and Week B; only the loading emphasis changes.

  • Week A — Slow Loading: one designated set of 5–10 controlled reps with approximately 4-second eccentrics and controlled transitions.
  • Week B — Isometric Loading: one challenging but tolerable 20–30-second hold at a useful joint angle.

This alternating pattern is a practical organizational choice, not evidence that alternation itself is superior. The goal is progressive tendon loading that fits within the existing workout. On deloads, remove special tendon work.

  • Monday: reverse Nordic (knee/rectus femoris) and standing calf raise (Achilles).
  • Wednesday: ATG split squat (knee) and supported external rotation (rotator cuff/shoulder).
  • Friday: Cossack (adductors/groin) and tibialis raise (shin/ankle).

Healthy shoulders need scapular excursion in rows and pulls, overhead control in handstands and presses, and cuff loading. Knees and ankles benefit from gradual deep-range, calf/soleus/tibialis, jump and sprint exposure. Landings and braking train force absorption. Respect symptoms and next-day response; never force tendon pain.

06Countering Prolonged SittingRestore underused movement options without assuming every seated person has the same tight or weak muscles.⌄

No separate tech-neck block. Rows, overhead work, bridges and well-executed suitcase carries support upper-body/postural capacity. Carries are not a direct neck treatment: keep the head neutral and shoulders level, without shrugging.

We do not diagnose shortened hip flexors, “inactive” glutes or posture problems simply because someone sits. Assess actual ROM, strength and control. Short walking/standing breaks during work complement training; the gym cannot fully replace daily movement exposure.

07Symmetry and IndividualizationMeasure left–right differences under standardized conditions; intervene only when the difference is meaningful and repeatable.⌄

How Asymmetry Changes Programming

Use a separate Left–Right Assessment every ~4–6 weeks. Film identical setup and loading for each side. Compare clean reps, depth/angle, pelvic/rib position, control and symptom response. A single awkward repetition or subjective “activation” difference is not proof of dysfunction.

Strength gap: start with the less capable side; have the stronger side match its clean reps/ROM. ROM gap: standardize setup and target controlled access before loading deeper. Control gap: reduce load or leverage until both sides own the same pattern. Persistent repeatable gap: consider one small targeted set if recovery allows. Pain/giving way: stop provocative testing and seek evaluation when warranted.

A ~10–15% repeatable difference may be worth investigating, but is not a diagnostic cutoff. See the dedicated assessment page for camera positions, individual test protocols, scenarios and adjustments.

08Weekly Architecture and RecoveryThree strength days, one light skill day, one athletic day, and two genuine rest days distribute stimulus without stacking fatigue.⌄

Why the 3 + 1 + 1 + 2 Structure Works

  • Monday — Stretch: fresh after Sunday rest; heavy long-length lower-body and pulling work with front-split and pike transfer.
  • Tuesday — Gymnastics + ROM: short, low-fatigue handstand, lever, bridge and active-ROM practice between heavy days.
  • Wednesday — Strength + Gymnastics: second strength exposure with inverted pressing, back development and efficient hip-extension strength.
  • Thursday — Rest: recover before Friday.
  • Friday — 360: lateral hip strength, rotation, muscle-up power and upper-body work, with moderate lower-body fatigue before sport.
  • Saturday — Athletic Conditioning: express strength through speed, coordination and repeat efforts.
  • Sunday — Rest: recover for Monday.

Recreational sport replaces a scheduled session, not a rest day. Ideally schedule it Saturday in place of sprint/agility. If sport is scheduled Tuesday, it replaces the lighter gymnastics/ROM session; move essential skill practice into short fresh blocks on strength days and make Saturday easy aerobic or recovery-based as appropriate. Thursday and Sunday remain rest days.

Other Saturday Choices: sprint and agility work for acceleration/deceleration; soccer or racket sports for multidirectional reactions; rowing, swimming or cycling for aerobic capacity (these do not fully replace landing/cutting skills). Avoid combining a hard sport session with an additional maximal sprint session by default.

09Progression and MeasurementPrioritize heavier clean core sets, then movement quality, total workload and aesthetics.⌄

Recommended KPI Hierarchy

  1. Primary — increase load on the heavy core sets, holding reps, full ROM, technique and RIR comparable. A heavier shallow rep does not count.
  2. Second — how the body feels and functions: mobility, posture, joints/tendons, freedom of movement and symmetry. Support subjective impressions with standardized ROM assessments.
  3. Third — total workload across core sets: track load × reps and completed quality work as a secondary signal, not something to maximize blindly. Compare like-for-like movements and unilateral conventions.
  4. Fourth — aesthetic gains: muscularity and proportions (rounded shoulders, glute shape, back thickness).

When To Add A Heavy Set

If progressing, change nothing. If stalled for ~3–4 comparable exposures, first check technique, ROM, loading increments, sleep, sport fatigue and soreness. If truly stalled and recovered, consider adding one heavy set to that specific lift. If tired, address fatigue instead. Preserve the smallest dose that keeps progress moving.

10Exercise EconomyMaximize adaptation per minute and unit of recoverable fatigue; every movement must earn its place.⌄

Why The Program Is Built This Way

DecisionRationale
Multifunctional compoundsOne movement can train strength, long-length hypertrophy, ROM and stability, reducing exercise count and recovery cost.
Noncompeting supersetsUpper/lower or unrelated patterns reduce idle time while preserving performance, provided heavy sets still get enough rest.
Strength → related ROMUse the preceding movement to prepare a relevant position, then practice the specific split/fold. Keep intermediate ROM exposure non-fatiguing before later heavy sets.
Keep targeted isolationRetain movements where compounds miss a meaningful function: lateral delts, rotator cuff, rectus femoris, hip flexors, tibialis.
Skills and power while freshHigh-speed force and precise motor learning deteriorate under fatigue; do not myo-rep or failure-train these.
Correct only measured gapsDo not build a giant corrective routine around assumed desk posture or one subjective left/right sensation.

The exercise filter: (1) Is the function already covered? (2) Can one movement develop strength and ROM together? (3) What capability disappears if we remove it? (4) Does the whole week still cover our goals? Economy is not minimalism for its own sake; it is high-value adaptation with recoverable fatigue.

Left–right symmetry & ROM assessment

A separate periodic assessment—not an additional weekly workout. Retest every 4–6 weeks.

Protocol

Test after the same short warm-up, on a reasonably fresh day—not immediately after hard sport or maximal leg work. Use the same equipment, load, stance, shoes, camera height, distance and lighting on both sides and at every retest.

Film 3–5 seconds before and after each effort, including setup and the whole body. Name clips YYYY-MM-DD · Test · L/R · View. Record reps, load, ROM, control, RIR and symptoms. Take still frames at the deepest/peak controlled position.

Decision rule

Repeatable ~10–15% differences can be a useful investigation flag—not a diagnosis or a universal cutoff. Start the less-capable side first, match the stronger side to its clean ROM/reps, and add a targeted set only when a real gap persists and recovery permits.

Pain, giving way, swelling, tingling or numbness: stop provocative testing; do not force symmetry.

01Deep Bulgarian split squatStrength · depth · pelvis⌄
Camera / photo

Front at hip height + side at hip height; full body, feet visible.

Standardized test

Same bench height, stance, DB load and tempo; perform 5–8 clean reps/side at ~2 RIR.

What to look for: Compare deepest controlled position, knee path, pelvic drop/rotation, heel contact, rep quality and RIR.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this
Fewer clean reps on one sideStart that side first; match other side’s clean reps and depth; progress the weaker side’s load only when quality matches.
Depth differs despite similar strengthStandardize foot distance/bench height; check ankle/hip contribution; use controlled ROM progression before adding weight.
Pelvis rotates, hip hikes or knee cavesReduce load and slow the eccentric; work within a stable depth; reassess before adding extra volume.
Pain, buckling or instabilityStop the provocative test; do not push to failure or force symmetry; obtain clinical assessment if persistent.
02Squared front splitLeft/right lead · sagittal hip ROM⌄
Camera / photo

True side view at hip height, one video with left lead and one with right; show pelvis and both legs.

Standardized test

Same warm-up and hand supports; square hips, front knee extended comfortably, rear hip directed forward; stop at controlled depth.

What to look for: Measure pelvis-to-floor distance; note pelvic opening, lumbar arching, rear knee bend, and whether front hamstring or rear anterior hip limits the position.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this
One lead leg remains much higherRepeat with identical pelvis square and hand support; bias controlled exposure to the limited configuration.
Rear hip cannot extend without lumbar archUse a shallower supported split and bridge/hip-extension progression; avoid substituting low-back arch for hip extension.
Front knee bends or pelvis rotates to gain depthRecord the honest depth and train controlled hamstring/hip access rather than forcing the lower number.
Pinching or nerve-like symptomsReduce depth and stop forcing the position; seek evaluation for persistent symptoms.
03Weighted Cossack squatAdductors · lateral hip · ankle⌄
Camera / photo

Front view at hip height with feet, knees and pelvis in frame; add side angle if heel lift occurs.

Standardized test

Use identical stance width, toe angle, DB load and tempo; 5–8 controlled reps each direction.

What to look for: Compare pelvis depth, heel contact, knee tracking, trunk rotation, adductor tension and control out of the bottom.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this
One direction is shallowerReduce load, standardize stance, work controlled depth; compare ankle dorsiflexion and adductor tolerance before assuming cause.
Heel lifts on one sideCheck stance/toe angle and ankle range; use a tolerable heel setup while progressively building control.
Pelvis shifts/rotates or knee collapsesUse slower reps and a lighter load; keep pelvis/foot tripod consistent.
Same ROM but different muscle sensationLog it without inventing a correction; compare actual performance over multiple tests.

Hold a support, same ankle load; stand tall, ribs stacked, bend knee ~90°, lift without leaning back; 8–12 clean reps/side.

What to look for: Compare peak thigh angle, pelvic hike, torso lean, control at the top and clean rep count.

Hip pinching or groin painShorten range and stop forcing compression; reassess before adding load.
05Single-leg calf raiseGastrocnemius/Achilles · ankle control⌄
Camera / photo

Side view for heel height; rear view for heel alignment and ankle collapse; barefoot or same shoes.

Standardized test

Same step/flat-floor setup and fingertip support; straight knee; 10–20 full-height controlled reps per side.

What to look for: Compare full heel height, rep count at that height, tempo, foot tripod, ankle drift and symptom response.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this
One side loses height earlierCount only full-height reps; start weaker side and cap stronger side at the same clean number.
Ankle rolls or arch collapsesReduce load; control foot tripod and alignment before raising intensity.
Achilles pain/swellingDo not force repeated loaded reps or end-range holds; adjust load and seek evaluation if persistent.
06Overhead shoulder reachShoulder flexion · scapular motion · rib control⌄
Camera / photo

Side view at shoulder height plus front view; capture both arms individually and together.

Standardized test

Stand with ribs stacked, pelvis neutral, elbows straight, thumbs up; slowly reach overhead without forcing.

What to look for: Compare upper-arm angle, rib flare/lumbar arch, scapular upward rotation, shrug asymmetry and elbow bend.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this

07Half-kneeling trunk rotationThoracic rotation · rib/pelvis separation⌄
Camera / photo

Front/three-quarter view with hips and shoulders visible; repeat same camera and kneeling side.

Standardized test

Half-kneel, pelvis facing front; rotate torso slowly both ways with arms crossed; test both kneeling configurations.

What to look for: Compare shoulder-line rotation relative to pelvis, hip shift, rib flare, lumbar side-bend and discomfort.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this
One rotation direction smallerKeep pelvis quiet; practice controlled chops/rotations in the limited direction without forcing range.
Pelvis turns with the shouldersReduce rotation range and load; train rib/pelvis dissociation.
Pain or catchingStop the provocative direction and assess rather than adding aggressive twisting.
08Full gymnastics bridgeIntegrated hip · thoracic · shoulder extension⌄
Camera / photo

True side view and view from head/feet along the body; capture full position and entry/exit.

Standardized test

After warm-up, use a safe regression (elevated hands or supported bridge) if needed; only attempt floor bridge when comfortable and controlled.

What to look for: Look for even hand/foot pressure, elbow lock as tolerated, shoulder opening, hip height/extension, symmetry, and excessive localized lumbar hinging.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this
Hips remain flexed despite adequate shoulder openingTrain rear-hip extension/front split and glute-driven hip extension; progress bridge height gradually.
Arms cannot open without lumbar archUse elevated-hand bridge and overhead shoulder/thoracic progressions; do not force a floor bridge.
Weight shifts or torso twistsUse a simpler supported bridge, center load and retest left/right support.
Back/shoulder pain or neurological symptomsStop and avoid forcing the position; seek assessment if symptoms persist.
09Suitcase carryLateral trunk · gait · shoulder/pelvic symmetry⌄
Camera / photo

Front and rear walking video, camera ~hip/chest height; record 20–30 m or 30–45 sec each side.

Standardized test

Use the same DB load, route, pace and distance; switch hands; walk tall with neutral head and relaxed, level shoulders.

What to look for: Compare side-bend, shoulder hiking, pelvic drop, stride length, grip failure and neck tension.

Observation → programming adjustment

If you see…Do this
Leans more with load on one sideReduce weight until upright; train equal-distance carries with clean posture, then add load.
One shoulder hikes or neck tightensLower load and relax shoulder without collapsing; avoid turning carries into a shrug.
One side’s grip fails firstTrack grip separately from trunk quality; use shorter bouts to maintain the intended trunk stimulus.
Pain or numbnessStop the provoking load; investigate rather than pushing through.
